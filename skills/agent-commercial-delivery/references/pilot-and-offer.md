# Offer and pilot

Enter at the current stage: segment/problem → process/alternatives → bounded
offer → relevant demo → agreed pilot → acceptance → recorded deal → handover →
use and effect. These states may overlap in time; keep their evidence distinct.

A useful offer explains who it serves, which work it performs, when it needs a
person, what systems/data it requires and what evidence supports its claims.
Describe the result in the buyer's language. Avoid unlimited autonomy or absolute
reliability claims unsupported by the operating contract.

For a pilot, identify the real participants, authorized channel and data,
baseline workload, supported scenarios, critical failures, costs/time limit,
human fallback, stop/rollback and acceptance owner. Existing approvals persist;
only newly consequential unknowns require a decision. Do not force a questionnaire
or invent financial/legal terms to complete a template.

Demonstrate a scenario relevant to the customer's work, including a meaningful
exception. Check actual saved/output/delivery evidence instead of a narrated
success. Use synthetic data until real-data access is authorized. Real provider
effects need the appropriate scope; a demo is not authorization for outbound sales.

Economics: define successful process completion, then include costs of successful
and failed attempts, retries, human intervention, tools, hosting/observation,
maintenance and onboarding over the measured period. If no process completed,
cost per success is undefined, not zero. Separate observed time savings from
unproven assumptions about future load, salary savings or revenue.

Public benchmarks support test design, not customer performance promises. State
the actual version, workload and bounds of any product claim. General offer and
channel work stays with existing business/marketing owners; this skill adds the
agent's evidence and operational limits.
