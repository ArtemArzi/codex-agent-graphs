# Application and effect after a change

## Establish what actually changed

Read the original expected behavior/outcome and existing task/release evidence.
Identify the actual product surface, intended people, and the change they must
encounter. For software, establish release/artifact/target identity using its
native owner; for a process or document, establish that the new procedure/result
was delivered and used by the intended recipient.

A merge, local test, publication request, elapsed soak time, or owner's statement
alone does not prove that the intended change reached people. State estimates as
estimates. When release or application cannot be established, report that gap
before evaluating effect; continue independently useful permitted observations.

## Compare the relevant work

Define a suitable observation window from the scenario's actual frequency and
client/process adoption delay. Retain project targets and quality tolerances;
do not invent a universal daily/weekly window or minimum sample threshold.

Check the measured event's semantics and denominator. Compare compatible periods,
cohorts, versions, instrumentation and exposure; note seasonality, outages and
other interventions. Use a relevant unaffected segment or direct episode evidence
when available. Name confidence and missing evidence; causal attribution requires
more than a favorable before/after difference.

Distinguish the window for attributing a regression from the time needed to see
an intended outcome. A later release/process change touching the same path can
confound the comparison. Preserve separately attributable evidence; do not force
a single cause for the whole later period.

Re-measure the change's claim and check important side effects: unfinished work,
new errors, quality, manual assistance, waiting, and total effort across roles.
The appropriate evidence may be a few observed work episodes rather than product
analytics. Do not invent users, interviews, counts, measured savings or revenue.

## State what the evidence supports

Use the project's existing vocabulary to distinguish:

- The change is not confirmed live/delivered, or its application is unknown.
- People encountered/used it, but evidence of the intended outcome is insufficient.
- The observed outcome supports the claim within the stated scope and confidence.
- The observation contradicts the claim or shows a material side effect.

No traffic, absent collection, or a failed source is inconclusive. Zero errors
without exposure is not a verified fix. A positive review, login, or received
artifact is not automatically useful application or lasting business effect.

Link the verdict to the existing work. Keep technical completion and follow-up
evidence separate: unknown business effect does not invalidate unchanged technical
checks, and valid technical checks do not complete the business hypothesis.

If more observation is useful, record the next source/condition/window and owner
without pretending a reminder exists. A failed expectation or recurring incident
routes through the current owner with new evidence. Only the necessary affected
checks are repeated; follow-up is not another full release suite.
